Geometry of interfaces

A rounded rectangle is specified by one number, yet its corner involves four independent decisions: how deep the corner is cut, how far along the edge the curve begins, how the curvature is distributed, and how an inner contour follows an outer one. This study separates those decisions, measures them, and sorts each statement into one of three kinds: geometry that can be proved, perception that has to be tested, and artistic choice.

Lengths are given in units of r, the radius of the circular corner taken as reference. The atlas draws the same constructions as plates, and the laboratory applies them to working components.

A corner is a transition

A circular corner joins two straight edges with a quarter circle. Tangents agree at the joins, so the contour has no kink: it is continuous in direction, which geometers call G1. Its curvature is not continuous. It is zero on the edge and 1/r on the arc, with a step between them. A contour whose curvature also varies without a step is called G2.

Three constructions remove the step. A superellipse flattens the curve near the joins. An Euler spiral, whose curvature grows in proportion to the distance travelled, can lead into a shorter circular arc and out of it again; Raph Levien's thesis is the reference treatment of that curve. A single cubic Bézier with both handles placed on the vertex also starts and ends with zero curvature, and is the simplest of the three to write.

These curves cannot be compared at equal radius, because the radius does not mean the same thing for each of them. They are compared here at equal cut: every curve passes through the same point of the diagonal, 0.41 r from the vertex.

Two consequences follow, and both are arithmetic.

Continuity is paid for in length and in sharpness. A corner that starts from zero curvature must turn the same quarter turn, so it turns harder in the middle and begins earlier on the edge. The superellipse takes 84% more of the edge than the arc it replaces. On a control whose height is less than 3.7 r, two such corners no longer fit on the short side.

At control sizes the difference is below a pixel. Once the cut is matched, the superellipse never departs from the circular corner by more than 0.028 r: 0.23 CSS pixel for an 8 px corner, 0.45 for 16 px, 0.91 for 32 px. The spiral blend stays within 0.19 px at 16 px. On a button or a field, a continuous corner changes a few antialiased pixels. On a card, a sheet or a photograph it changes a visible band.

Whether observers see that difference, and from which size, is not established by any source consulted for this study. It is a hypothesis to test, not a result.

One number for the curve

A superellipse corner satisfies |x/a|ⁿ + |y/b|ⁿ = 1. The exponent n sets the distribution of curvature: 2 gives the ellipse, larger values push the curve towards the vertex, 1 gives a straight bevel.

CSS Borders and Box Decorations Level 4, a W3C working draft, exposes this as corner-shape: superellipse(K), where the exponent is 2ᴷ. That reading is the one consistent with the keywords the draft defines: bevel is superellipse(0), round is superellipse(1), squircle is superellipse(2), and negative values mirror the curve into scoop and notch. The value interpolates, so a corner can animate from one shape to another.

The property keeps border-radius as the box in which the corner is drawn. A squircle with the radius of a round corner is therefore cut 0.22 r deep where the round one is cut 0.41 r: it reads as a smaller radius. Much of what is attributed to the smoothness of an uncompensated squircle is a change of size.

The two can be separated. A corner of curve K keeps the cut of a round corner of radius r when its radius is multiplied by

(1 − 2^(−1/2)) / (1 − 2^(−1/2ᴷ))

which gives 1.35 for K = 1.5, 1.84 for K = 2 and 2.54 for K = 2.5. The factor is a pure function of K and can be written in CSS with pow(). Matching the removed area instead of the cut gives 1.72 for K = 2; which of the two looks equal is a question for an experiment.

corner-shape shipped in Chrome and Edge 139. Firefox and Safari do not implement it at the time of writing, and MDN lists it as limited availability. Borders, outlines, shadows and overflow clipping follow the shaped contour, which clip-path and masks cannot offer: they cut the border, the shadow and the focus ring away with the rest.

Nested contours

For an inset d smaller than a circular corner's radius R, a concentric arc of radius R − d gives a constant gap. A parallel contour can also keep a constant gap inside a superellipse. Construct it by moving a distance d along the inward normal at each point, as shown in the atlas.

  • Equal circular radii leave a diagonal gap of √2 d in the example, 41% wider than on the edges.
  • Radius minus inset keeps the gap constant for circular corners. When the inset reaches the radius, the inner corner is square.
  • Superellipses do not nest by subtraction. An inner squircle of radius R − d leaves a diagonal gap 19% wider than d at d = R/4 and 14% wider at d = R/2. The exact inner contour is not a superellipse; the draft specification says as much, and browsers compute it for borders themselves.

Elliptical corners behave the same way. For a corner of 1.5rem / 0.625rem, subtracting an inset of 0.3125rem from both radii leaves a gap that narrows by 13% along the curve.

Optical corrections

Geometry gives candidates; it does not say which one looks right.

A circle inscribed in a square covers 79% of its area and looks smaller. The circle of equal area has a diameter of 2/√π, or 1.128 times the side. The keylines of Material's system icons use a 20 dp circle with an 18 dp square, a ratio of 1.111, close to equal area and rounded to the grid. A triangle centred on its bounding box sits one sixth of its height away from its centroid, which is why play glyphs are shifted towards their point.

Equal area and centroid alignment are demonstrable constructions. That they match perceived size and perceived centre is a working rule of practitioners, to be checked for each glyph.

What the references do

Apple offers two corner curves on a layer, circular and continuous, and SwiftUI adds concentric shapes whose radius is the container's radius minus the distance between the corners, with an optional minimum. The continuous curve is not published as a formula. Figma's analysis of the iOS icon found that no superellipse reproduces it and that its curvature profile is assembled from several Bézier segments; Figma's own corner smoothing joins a circular arc to the edges with Bézier transitions. Nesting is solved in the layout system, where the distance is known.

Material Design treats shape as a polygon with rounded vertices that can morph into another polygon, through the androidx.graphics.shapes library. The contribution is motion between shapes; its corners remain circular arcs with an optional smoothing parameter.

Fluent 2 publishes a short radius scale, from 2 px to 12 px and the circle, and ties the step to the size of the element. It does not state a rule for nested corners.

Carbon builds on a 2x grid and keeps rectangular geometry; its icon guidelines specify a 2 px corner. No corner-radius token was found in its documentation.

Three observations hold across them. Curvature continuity is adopted where surfaces are large and hardware is curved, and ignored for small controls, which agrees with the pixel arithmetic above. Nesting needs the distance between the contours, so it belongs to layout and not to a static token. None of these systems publishes perceptual evidence for its corner curve.

On perception, the literature supports less than is often claimed. Bar and Neta reported a preference for curved over sharp-cornered objects. Bertamini and colleagues found that the effect is an attraction to curvature and not an avoidance of angles. Both concern object contours, not the choice between two rounded corners of different continuity.

Rules for a design system

Five rules follow from the sections above. Each one is decided by measuring the contours a system draws: it holds or it is broken, on every contour, and a contour it does not constrain cannot break it.

  1. A curve keeps the cut of the radius scale. A radius fixes how deep a corner is cut; the distribution of curvature is a second parameter. A contour drawn with a curve other than the arc has its radius multiplied by the factor given above, so that it cuts as deep as a step of the scale.
  2. A continuous curve is used where it can be seen. At equal cut, a continuous corner that stays within one device pixel of the circular one is drawn as an arc: the curve changes nothing there and takes more of the edge.
  3. Inlaid arcs share a centre. For an inner circular corner that sits the same distance from both edges and inside the outer arc, use max(minimum, outer radius − inset). With elliptical corners, subtracting the inset on each axis aligns the centres and the gap varies. A container can pad its content clear of its corner curves. The minimum radius is an artistic decision.
  4. A rounded contour is drawn by its border, not by a clip. A contour drawn with corner-shape carries the border, the shadow and the focus outline with it, and falls back to an arc where the property is missing. A clip path or a mask removes all three.
  5. Two steps of a scale are a pixel apart, or they are one step. A scale offers a choice, and a choice no screen can draw is not one. Steps derived from a shape by a ratio collapse when the shape is fine: half and three quarters of a 3 px radius are 0.75 px apart. A system that derives its steps measures them on the contours they are meant for, and falls back to steps that can be told apart.

Open questions

Two statements of this study are not rules, because no measurement of a contour decides them.

  • Optical corrections. Equal area and centroid alignment are sound starting constructions for a glyph. Whether they look right is a matter of perception, untested here.
  • The default curve. A paired comparison of circular and continuous corners at equal cut, across sizes and pixel densities, would turn the central hypothesis of this page into a result. Until one exists, the circular arc is the default that can be defended.

Method and limits

Curves are sampled as polylines of 1,600 segments per corner; curvature is the inverse radius of the circle through three consecutive samples. The spiral blend spends half of its turn in the two spirals. Gaps are the distance from each sample of the inner contour to the nearest point of the outer one.

The study measures contours, not rendered pixels: antialiasing, stroke alignment and subpixel layout are outside its scope. Statements about Apple's continuous corner rely on Apple's API documentation and on Figma's published analysis, not on a specification. No experiment with observers has been run.
