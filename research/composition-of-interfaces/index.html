Composition of interfaces

Open a record, compare two recordings, then return to the collection. At the same window width, the comparison can have room for two passages while the record still needs its note below the text. What can hold these views together when they rearrange at different moments?

The three views above share six columns and the same margins. The useful difference lies in what each piece of content needs from those columns. A passage needs a sustained line of type. A note needs an edge near the passage it describes. The collection needs room for a succession of names. The worked series derives the different widths at which each arrangement becomes possible.

A grid supplies positions to return to across a composition. Its geometry can be specified exactly. Whether it helps a reader compare two entries requires observation. Which entry gets the larger field, and where the designer leaves space around it, are artistic decisions.

Establish the field

Draw the final frame, then mark the space the composition can occupy. On a bound spread the inner margins meet at the binding. On a folded sheet a crease may pass through otherwise available space. On screen the frame may be one panel within a larger page. Measure that panel. A marginal note outside the text block still needs to fit inside the frame.

If the frame width is W and its two side margins are L and R, the available width is A = W − L − R. Moving four units from the left margin to the right preserves the measure of every column but moves every leading edge four units to the left. This is one way to change a page's relationship to a facing page without resetting its text.

A single text block inside these margins already makes a manuscript grid. Set the body first and read several complete lines. Try a long word, a short final line and a second paragraph. The same physical measure gives different line breaks when typeface, size, language or word spacing changes. For a book, place two facing pages together before fixing the inner and outer margins.

A proportional grid for facing pages

Take a page of width W and height H. Reserve W/9 beside the binding and 2W/9 at the outer edge. Leave H/9 above the text and 2H/9 below. The text rectangle measures 2W/3 × 2H/3, so it keeps the page's aspect ratio and occupies four ninths of its area. Mirror the horizontal margins on the facing page. Across an open, flat spread the two inner margins add up to one outer margin. The deeper margin sits below the text on both pages.

On a 180 × 270 mm page the text block is 120 × 180 mm. Increase the page width to 210 mm and this construction gives the text 140 mm. If the type and line breaks of the original edition are worth keeping, retain the 120 mm measure and distribute the extra 30 mm in the margins. These are distinct briefs. One preserves proportions, the other preserves the setting of the text.

Construct the columns

For N equal columns and a uniform gutter G, there are N − 1 gutters. Subtract their total width before dividing the available field. With one column the entire available width belongs to it.

  • Each column has width C = (A − (N − 1)G) / N.
  • A block spanning k consecutive columns has width S(k) = kC + (k − 1)G.
  • Numbering from one, column j starts at x(j) = L + (j − 1)(C + G) in a left-to-right field.

The construction needs positive column width, an integer N ≥ 1 and a span from one through N. A block spanning two columns takes the gutter inside its footprint. A separate neighbouring block leaves the gutter between them open. The last column must end at W − R.

Take a 240-unit frame with 18 units on either side. The live field is 204 units wide. Six columns with five 6-unit gutters leave 174 units for type and artwork, or 29 per column. Two blocks spanning three columns each are 99 units wide. Giving one block two columns and the other four yields widths of 64 and 134, with the same 6-unit gutter. A 2:4 division in column count therefore produces a slightly wider than 2:1 ratio between the actual blocks.

The gutter has a cost. Double it from 6 to 12 while keeping the frame, margins and six columns. Each column shrinks from 29 to 24. The note spanning two columns loses four units of measure (64 to 60), while the passage spanning four loses only two (134 to 132). Set a full line on each side of the gap and check whether the two passages remain distinct and both still read well.

A six-column grid can carry halves, thirds and sixths without moving its guides. A four-column grid can carry quarters and halves. Choose between them by listing the arrangements a series actually needs. An essay and an occasional pair of examples may need only two columns. Adding guides that no element uses introduces choices without adding a usable alignment.

Give the spans a purpose

For sustained reading, give the text a continuous measure. A heading may use the same leading edge and span a greater width. Keep its line endings independent of the body so that a long title can wrap without widening every paragraph.

For explanation beside a reference, let the narrower region contain the shorter material, such as dates, notes or an index. Give their common edge a specific job. Follow a note across that edge to its corresponding passage. If the relationship repeatedly crosses a page turn, the rail needs a local reference or a different position. An asymmetric grid can be made with unequal spans on equal columns, as the column plate shows.

For a collection, repeat a set of fields for items of equal rank. An exceptional item can span two fields, but decide what warrants that emphasis and where the next ordinary item resumes. Empty modules can also carry meaning. Leaving a column open beside a title gives it room. Leaving the same column empty between a label and its value separates the two.

Massimo Vignelli gives a precise example in The Vignelli Canon. A three-column letterhead reserves the first column for an identifying mark or open space. The letter occupies the other two. A fold supplies another alignment in the vertical direction. In a second design he uses five columns for a centred heading and a three-column text block. The count follows the placement the designer wants to repeat. The Vignelli Canon, “Grids, Margins, Columns and Modules” and “A Company Letterhead”.

What does an empty column bring together?

A departure board has three things to say about each service. Its time occupies one column, its destination two, and its platform two. On our six-column ruling that leaves one column empty. Both settings below give each field the same width. They make different pairs out of the same words.

In the first setting, time and destination share a 6-unit gutter. Before the platform come a gutter, an empty 29-unit column and another gutter, 41 units in all. Move the empty column to the other side of the destination and the gaps exchange places. The outside edges, occupied area and number of alignments cannot tell the two settings apart. Which space to leave open depends on which two pieces someone needs to read together. These gaps are measured between fields. A short name such as Dry Dock leaves more space between its last letter and the next field.

Leyssen and colleagues asked people to compose or choose pictures of familiar objects. Participants preferred related objects closer together than unrelated ones. A preference for centring emerged most clearly when the distance within the pair was fixed. Their subject was pictorial preference. For the board this suggests a testable question. Would someone starting from a departure time find its destination faster in the first setting? Would someone starting from a destination find its platform faster in the second? Each question has a different starting point and a measurable answer. Leyssen et al., Aesthetic Preference for Spatial Composition in Multi-Object Pictures (2012).

Set modules and baselines

Columns describe horizontal positions. Add rows when material needs a shared top, bottom or recurring entry point across columns. A module is the field at the intersection of a column and a row. A baseline grid supplies the lines on which type can sit. Its step can be much finer than a module.

In the module plate the first horizontal guide sits at 16 and successive guides are 8 units apart. A module three steps deep is 24 units high. Leave one step between modules and the row starts are 16, 48, 80 and 112. An item spanning two rows occupies 24 + 8 + 24 = 56 units. The final row ends at 136 in the 160-unit frame, leaving 24 units below. The lower margin is a choice visible in the construction.

To align type across columns, set the first actual baseline and the advance to the next one. In the plate both columns start at 16. With advances of 16 on the left and 24 on the right, the two sequences meet again at 64 and 112. The repeat interval is 48, the least common multiple of the two advances. Giving two text boxes the same top edge and line height alone cannot guarantee the same first baseline for different fonts. Check where the letters sit inside the rendered line boxes.

A printed catalogue can allocate a second row to a long entry or continue it on the next page. On screen a description may grow after the reader opens it or enlarges the type. Let the row grow and resume the rhythm below it. A fixed module that clips a description preserves the arithmetic at the cost of the content. An alternative construction derives the vertical unit from the chosen leading and divides the field afterwards. IBM Design Language, baseline unit.

Find where the grid stops fitting

Record a minimum usable width for each role after setting representative material. Suppose a note requires 60 units and the main passage 100. These are design constraints for this example. In the six-column construction the proposed note spans two columns, so its width is 2C + 6. The note fits when C ≥ 27. The passage spans four columns and needs 4C + 18 ≥ 100, which gives C ≥ 20.5. The note therefore sets the limit.

With the original margins and gutters, the smallest frame for this allocation is 18 + 6 × 27 + 5 × 6 + 18 = 228 units. There the two blocks measure 60 and 126. At 222 units they measure 58 and 122. The main passage still fits and the note has already failed. A narrower setting can bring the note into the text flow and give both elements a single column. A different gutter or span would give a different threshold.

Choose the narrow reading order before moving the regions. Keep a note with the passage it explains, a heading with the section it introduces, and a label with its value. For interactive work, follow the focus sequence as well as reading the words. For ordinary vertically scrolling text, also check reflow at 320 CSS pixels. WCAG 2.2, Meaningful Sequence · Focus Order · Reflow.

One ruling for a family of views

Consider a small sound archive. Its record view sets a passage beside a field note. Its comparison gives two recordings equal space. Its index repeats an entry three times across the field. Begin with the divisions these tasks require. Halves and thirds can both end on the lines of six equal columns. Six is the smallest count divisible by both two and three. If quarters must also align across views, the smallest count becomes twelve. A quarter used only inside a single entry can be a local division.

The margins and gutters matter as much as the count. In the 240-unit construction above, a four-column field is 134 units wide. Divide it in two with the original 6-unit gutter and each half is 64 units. The halves recover the edges of the underlying two-column spans. With a local gutter of 2 units, the halves become 66 units. The centre of the gutter stays at 67, but each of its edges moves by 2. For a caption that must line up with the next row, the distinction is visible at the first letter.

Two working systems show different reasons to keep an edge across a series. In the US National Park Service's Unigrid, the folds define 4 × 8¼ inch panels. A brochure can be one or two panels wide and up to six panels long. The folded format and the size of a press sheet constrain the family while each park can give its map a different extent. National Park Service, A Brief History of the Unigrid.

In the GOV.UK Design System, the main text can keep its two-thirds measure both on pages with secondary material and on pages where the last third is empty. Here the repeated decision concerns the reading column. The space at its side is available to the composition without obliging an editor to fill it. GOV.UK Design System, Layout.

Different views change at different widths

For the archive, take a margin of 2 rem on each side and a gutter of 1 rem. One rem is the size of the root type on screen. Let the passage occupy four columns and the field note two. The editor gives the passage a measure between 23 and 33 rem and reserves at least 12 rem for the note. These are settings for this specimen. A different face, language or task may call for other measures.

At frame width W, the width of a span is S(k) = (k/N)(W − L − R + G) − G. Running the equation in reverse gives the frame at which a span first reaches a chosen measure m. The result is W = L + R − G + (N/k)(m + G). Each occupied region sets its own limit. The widest of the required lower bounds tells us when the whole row fits. A maximum reading measure can also set an upper bound.

At 40 rem, half the field is 17.5 rem once the intervening gutter is allowed for. Two recordings fit side by side. A two-column note gets only 11⅓ rem. It belongs below the passage at this width. The index fits two entries on its first row and carries the third to the next. A single switch for all three views would give up a viable comparison or squeeze the note. The interface can keep the same outer edges through all three changes.

An interface often changes width without the window moving at all. Take a 56-rem workspace. Opening a 14-rem navigation rail with a 1-rem gap leaves 41 rem for the working view. The comparison still fits. The note moves below the record and the third index entry starts a new row. Closing the rail restores all three arrangements. The width that matters here can be measured between the sides of the working pane.

For this edition, the whole frame stops growing at 54 rem. At that width the four-column passage reaches its chosen maximum of 33 rem. Extra space falls equally outside the edition and the three views keep their common edges. Another edition could let the collection grow wider and cap only the text inside the reading view. Its index would gain space at the cost of that edge matching across views.

When a proportion has no solution

Suppose the note must now accommodate an 18-rem transcription. On the four-plus-two division the note reaches that measure at 60 rem, while the passage reaches its ceiling at 54 rem. No frame width meets both conditions. Doubling the ruling to twelve and assigning eight columns to the passage and four to the note leaves their widths exactly as before.

Changing the allocation does help. On two equal fields, a frame of 51 rem gives each side 23 rem. Both pieces fit, the first line of the passage starts on its usual edge, and the note moves inward by one column. Below 51 rem the note can follow its passage in reading order. Try the three views at the same width.

The width of an actual label

What does the proposed measure buy in type? Take the short label East landing microphone. A two-column slot in the 40-rem frame is 11⅓ rem wide, or about 181.3 pixels with the root type set to 16 pixels. The two settings below use that exact measure, a 1-rem type size, medium weight and zero added letterspacing. Only the typeface changes.

At that size the unbroken label measured 185.1 pixels in Instrument Sans and 177.2 in Roboto in our Chromium setting. The wider label East landing and harbour wall microphones measured 318.9 and 308.4 pixels. Neither fits on one line in the proposed 18 rem (288 pixels). If a line break is acceptable, the note can keep that measure. Keeping the label on one line changes the brief. Within a two-column span on this ruling, each extra unit of required label width costs three units of frame width. A different string or a different font file needs its own measurement. Reading speed would require a reader study.

What measurements can establish

Construction. Track widths, alignment coordinates, spans and the positions of horizontal guides can all be calculated. The plates use abstract units and ideal edges. A physical fold has thickness, and a rasterised line may occupy fractional pixels. Verify the final edge on the intended medium. Changing a display width while holding text size constant is a new layout condition. Uniformly scaling down a drawing preserves only its geometry.

Reading. Dyson and Kipping compared line lengths in screen reading. Their 100-character lines were read faster than 25-character lines, with no observed difference in comprehension. Readers nevertheless rated the 55-character lines easiest. A fastest line, a preferred line and a line that improves comprehension can be three different claims. The results apply to the texts, type and screen conditions studied. The Effects of Line Length and Method of Movement on Patterns of Reading from Screen (1998).

Grouping. Brumby and Zhuang asked 36 people to find named words in menus. When the words were randomly arranged, adding boxes around groups increased mean correct search time from 3.95 to 4.51 seconds. Their items stayed in place. Widening a gutter moves items as well as separating them. The result gives a reason to check the meaning of a group before drawing its boundaries and leaves the effective gutter for any new layout open. Visual Grouping in Menu Interfaces (2015).

Art direction. The 3+3 and 2+4 arrangements both close exactly at the outer margins. Use equal spans when the items deserve equivalent treatment, or deliberately give one more space when it introduces the other. Arithmetic can reveal an unintended offset. It cannot tell an editor which passage deserves the emphasis.

What a layout system can carry from one view to the next

Give a recurring edge a name in the composition. In the archive, the record title, the first listening position and the first collection entry start on the same line. The far edge of the passage is free to change. A designer can now draw a fourth view and say exactly which edge it inherits. A locally divided field can meet the parent lines again when it inherits their gutter as well as their extent.

Specify the relationship each arrangement serves. Two equal spans give the recordings equivalent treatment. The unequal record gives the passage more room and keeps its note nearby. On the board, the open column separates one pair of fields so that the other pair sits together. Translating the words or reversing the reading direction can preserve those relationships even as the physical positions change.

Write a condition for leaving the arrangement. Set representative words in the intended face, work back from each required measure, and keep the intersection of the resulting ranges. If the intersection is empty, choose a different division or put the items in reading order on separate rows. This is where a family can share a drawing rule and still have different transitions. Six columns, 2-rem margins and a 54-rem ceiling describe one edition of the archive. The reasoning also works for another edition with different type and different dimensions.

How far from a passage should its note sit?

In the six-column example, arithmetic tells us the smallest width at which the note fits. It leaves another question open. A narrow gutter can make the note look like a continuation of the passage. A wide one can draw it towards the next passage. Which separation helps the reader match the note to the right text?

One comparison would keep the page size, type and line lengths fixed. The extra width for the gutter would come equally from the two outer margins. Readers would match notes to passages, encountering each passage only once. Reversing the order of the layouts and exchanging the passages between them would help distinguish the effect of the layout from the difficulty of a particular text.

Correct matches would tell us about reading. A separate preference judgement would tell us which page readers liked. Readers might prefer the more open page and make the same number of correct matches in both. The question remains open for these layouts.
